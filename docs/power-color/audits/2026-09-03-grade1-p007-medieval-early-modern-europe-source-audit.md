## Resolved narrow facts
The following nonvisual facts are independently auditable from museum collection/conservation authority and may be used only as narrow historical-color knowledge:

1. Medieval manuscript illuminators used pigments derived from plants, minerals and metals; some pigments were produced by grinding colored earths or semiprecious stones and mixing the powder with a binding medium, while others involved chemical separation or synthesis. Authority: J. Paul Getty Museum, `The Alchemy of Color in Medieval Manuscripts` / exhibition material.
2. Getty identifies ultramarine blue, vermilion red and orpiment yellow among pigments used by medieval manuscript illuminators. Authority: J. Paul Getty Museum, `Getty Presents The Alchemy of Color in Medieval Manuscripts`.
3. The Metropolitan Museum of Art documents ultramarine in the medieval `Belles Heures` manuscript and states that it was made from the semiprecious stone lapis lazuli. Authority: Metropolitan Museum of Art, `The Art of Illumination`, folio 136r.
4. National Gallery collection records for fifteenth-century Italian painting explicitly document expensive ultramarine made from lapis lazuli; for Lorenzo Monaco's `The Coronation of the Virgin`, lapis-lazuli blue is described as more expensive than gold, while gilding and vermilion are also documented in the work. Authority: National Gallery, London.
